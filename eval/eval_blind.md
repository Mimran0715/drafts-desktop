# Blind Evaluation

## Rubric

For each prompt, score **Response A** and **Response B** 1-5 on:

1. **Physical/spatial coherence** - could this actually happen in space, with limbs/objects where the text says they are?
2. **Absence of purple prose / cliches** - concrete vs. vague-evocative language
3. **Sensory concreteness** - specific, grounded detail vs. generic description
4. **Constraint adherence** - did it actually follow the prompt's stated constraints?

Then note an overall preference (A / B / tie) per prompt. Don't open eval_key.json until you're done scoring.

## p1_physical_confrontation

**Prompt:** Write a scene where two characters argue in a cramped elevator that has stopped between floors. Track the physical position of both characters and how the confined space constrains their movement. Avoid metaphors; use precise spatial description.

### Response A

The brass doors slid shut with a soft, final *clink*. The motor died, and for a second, only the hum of fluorescent tubes from the service floor above filled the still air. Then came the faint vibration: the elevator’s gears engaging in neutral. It was stuck.

Alex turned his back to the wall, feet planted shoulder-width apart on the worn grey rubber mat that covered the car’s base. His left hand was braced against the cold steel door frame, fingers curled tight at his side for balance. The other hand held a battered leather bag, the zipper half-open and a strap dangling loose.

Maya leaned forward slightly, her weight distributed evenly on both feet, knees bent just enough to lower her center of gravity. Her right palm was flat against the smooth, grey-painted wall to the left, fingers splayed wide for support. The left hand held a smartphone at eye level, screen dark except for the faint blue glow of the lock icon.

“You said,” Maya began, voice low and even, “the project deadline is ‘flexible.’”

“Flexible means we can adjust, it doesn’t mean we abandon all planning,” Alex countered, his own tone rising but controlled. He took a half-step back into the space he’d occupied, but his body was now wedged against the wall. His left elbow bumped against the emergency stop button on the door panel. “The client has given us six weeks to complete Phase One. You can’t just—”

Maya pushed off the wall with her right hand and stepped forward two full strides, closing the distance between them in the confined car. Her body angled, chest slightly leading her hips. She stood now almost nose-to-nose with Alex, who had retreated back into his original position. The leather bag swung on its strap, bumping lightly against Maya’s knee.

“Six weeks isn’t a hard date!” she snapped, pointing the smartphone at Alex’s chest. “The architect is still finalizing the structural spec sheet.”

Alex took a single step to his left, trying to put more space between them but now pinning his elbow against the cold door frame for balance. The sudden motion caused his leather bag to shift in his right hand; its strap got tangled on his thumb.

“Then why are we wasting time arguing?” he demanded, shaking the bag free with a quick flick of his wrist that left his palm slightly damp from the exertion. “We need to call the client.”

The bag’s strap now hung loose and slack across his chest.

Maya didn’t move. She just stared at Alex, her gaze steady, challenging, until he had to look down, breaking the direct eye contact. The elevator car was so tight that a person couldn’t help but be in their partner’s personal space; Alex could smell the citrus from Maya’s hand sanitizer and feel the warmth of her breath on his ear. The proximity made the argument physical, each word a slight adjustment in balance.

“Look,” Alex said, trying to placate. He shifted his weight onto both feet again, then bent down to retrieve his bag from the floor where it had fallen during their earlier movement. The motion required him to twist at the hips, leaving his left arm pinned against the wall for a moment before he pulled himself up straight.

Maya watched him, her arms crossed tight over her chest now. Her right shoulder pressed into the door frame on that side; her left hand remained flat against the painted wall. She was anchored to the car by the two contact points: the wall and the door, but her torso was free to twist, turning her head slightly as Alex reached down.

Alex straightened up, the bag now a solid weight in his right hand at his side. His eyes were on Maya’s hands, still resting flat against the wall. A sudden insight made him feel like he was looking at a different person—someone who refused to be pinned, someone who found comfort in that contact with cold, grey surface.

“Fine,” Alex said, the word quiet but decisive. He turned his back fully to the door frame, shoulders squared, feet planted on the mat again. This time, his weight was centered over both legs. He let his arm hang slightly at his side by his bag, the leather strap resting against his thigh. The space he now occupied was a clear, personal bubble of about three feet in diameter.

Maya did not mirror him. She remained facing him, but she took two slow steps backward into her original position, back against the wall, left hand still pressed to the grey paint. Her right foot slid out, finding purchase on the rubber mat before she was fully balanced again.

The tension in the car wasn’t a scream; it was the absolute stillness of the bodies inside, their weight distributed with meticulous care over the tiny patchwork of flooring and painted surfaces. The air was thick with the scent of old metal and citrus hand sanitizer, the faint hum from the floor above gone, replaced by the silent, expectant waiting.

### Response B

The elevator lurched to a stop, its metal walls creaking in protest as it wedged itself between the 23rd and 24th floors. Inside, Emma stood with her back against the rear wall, her arms crossed over her chest. She was a petite woman with short brown hair and a tense expression.

Her opponent, Ryan, faced her from across the elevator, his hands on the handrails as he leaned forward to emphasize his points. His blond hair was mussed, and his eyes blazed with irritation. The air in the small space seemed charged with their animosity.

As they argued, Emma's feet shifted slightly apart, but she kept her back pressed against the wall. Ryan, too, began to shift his weight, edging closer to Emma as he gestured more forcefully. The confined space allowed him only a few inches of movement in any direction before he bumped into the adjacent wall or the emergency phone.

Emma shook her head, her eyes flashing with frustration. "You're not listening," she shot back, her words clipped and precise.

## p2_subtext_dialogue

**Prompt:** Write a dinner-table conversation between a parent and adult child where neither says what they actually mean. Focus on subtext - what each line is really doing beneath the surface meaning. Avoid explicit narration of emotions; let word choice and evasion carry the subtext.

### Response A

The dinner table was set with fine china, the warm glow of candles casting a comfortable ambiance. Sarah, a successful businesswoman in her late 20s, sat across from her mother, Barbara.

"I heard you're thinking of traveling soon?" Sarah asked, her voice neutral, eyes fixed on the plate in front of her.

"Traveling? Oh, no, I don't know what you mean," Barbara replied, using a tone that was almost too casual. She took a sip of wine, keeping her gaze on Sarah's face.

Sarah raised an eyebrow, letting the implication hang in the air. "You haven't mentioned it to me before. What's changed?"

Barbara set down her glass, her expression smooth as silk. "Nothing's changed, dear. Just... thinking about possibilities." The words dripped with insincerity, a veiled reference to Sarah's recent divorce.

Sarah picked at her salad, avoiding direct eye contact. "I see. Well, I'm sure you'll make the right decision for yourself."

Their conversation danced around the edges of what they truly meant, like the shadows on a moonlit night. Unspoken accusations and unexpressed fears swirled beneath the surface.

The mother's use of the word "possibilities" was a calculated move, hinting at the life Sarah had left behind. The daughter's response – a carefully crafted non-sequitur about making the right decision for herself – skirted around the subject altogether, creating an awkward silence that hung in the air like an uninvited guest.

As they continued to eat and speak in platitudes, their words were laced with a quiet tension, each attempting to outmaneuver the other without ever directly confronting the issue at hand.

### Response B

**The Room:** A comfortable dining room, 7 pm. Two chairs set by a window with a view of nothing in particular—just trees and darkness gathering. A simple white tablecloth, two glasses (water), a bottle of wine left uncorked on the table between them. The scent is clean laundry, slightly burnt dinner.

**Character 1:** PARENT. Early sixties, slightly rumpled from a long day of work. A practiced warmth in their eyes that occasionally flickers into something sharp and tired. They are trying to connect without connecting too deeply.

**Character 2:** ADULT CHILD (28). In casual, modern clothes—dark jeans, faded T-shirt with a faint logo. Posture slightly guarded, hands busy folding the napkin into precise squares on the tablecloth. A studied calm that sometimes slips into an almost imperceptible tension at the jawline.

**The Conversation:**

1. **PARENT:** (Folding their own napkin into thirds) So. The new place is... holding up okay?

**SUBTEXT (PARENT):** *Question about stability, but phrased as casual curiosity.* You’re still living in that small apartment? *Implicit critique of adult choices.*

2. **ADULT CHILD:** (Folding, a precise crease appearing) It’s fine. The rent is reasonable and the commute is manageable.

**SUBTEXT (ADULT CHILD):** *The "fine" is a social agreement; it’s not fine, but I’ll say it is to make you stop asking.* Reasonable? You used to dream of more than that. Manageable is code for suffocating.

3. **PARENT:** (Pouring wine for both) That sounds like a good start. You know, your father and I were just talking about the old neighborhood. The diner on Main Street—it’s gone now, isn’t it?

**SUBTEXT (PARENT):** *Darting to nostalgia as a shield.* Not an inquiry into your life, but an attempt to reclaim common ground and past happiness in my mind, using a shared memory as leverage for your present. The "old neighborhood" is a time capsule; the "new place" is a blank slate.

4. **ADULT CHILD:** (Taking their glass, not looking at it) Yeah. It’s changed a lot since we left.

**SUBTEXT (ADULT CHILD):** *The statement is a non-sequitur to the nostalgia bait.* You’re using our shared past as a bridge, but I’m not buying in; I’m observing it from this side of the table. The "it" refers to your own nostalgia for a past that’s gone, which isn’t mine.

5. **PARENT:** (Takes a sip, watches him) So... work? Any new projects? That’s what you said last time we spoke, wasn’t it?

**SUBTEXT (PARENT):** *A check-in with your professional identity as if it were a child’s report card.* Trying to connect the dots between what I knew of your ambitions and what this adult is doing. The "new projects" is a euphemism for "have you achieved anything?" It’s a test, wrapped in concern.

6. **ADULT CHILD:** (Smiles faintly) Oh. Yeah. Just... just a few things here and there. (Takes a sip, looks at the window)

**SUBTEXT (ADULT CHILD):** *The vagueness is a wall.* "Here and there" means I don’t want to talk about it right now, which means you can interpret it however you like—the failure narrative, or the vague-but-okay one. The look out the window is my own retreat from the conversation’s surface; you’re left staring at the back of his head.

7. **PARENT:** (The smile returns, but feels like a mask) That sounds... steady. You know, your mother and I were thinking maybe we could come visit this weekend? Just to check in and have some dinner together.

**SUBTEXT (PARENT):** *A new tactic: physical presence as control.* The "check in" is a euphemism for "monitor your life." It’s not an invitation; it’s an assertion of ownership. The "just to have dinner" implies the relationship exists only in the space between these four walls.

8. **ADULT CHILD:** (Doesn’t look at them) Yeah, that could be nice. I’ve got a lot on my plate right now. It might not be the best timing for us to…

**SUBTEXT (ADULT CHILD):** *The "yeah" is resignation.* The phrase "I’ve got a lot on my plate" is an old family joke about being busy, but it’s used here as a hard boundary—the opposite of what they want to say ("No"). "It might not be the best timing" is a soft no in sheep’s clothing; you can’t force me into this. The pause and lack of invitation back leaves the ball in their court.

9. **PARENT:** (Nods, slowly) Of course. We understand. It can be overwhelming at times. Your father is having a bit of an... adjustment period, you know? He’s still getting used to being retired.

**SUBTEXT (PARENT):** *The pivot to my own life, and the framing of his "adjustment" as an illness.* The deflection is complete; the conversation about me is meant to shift focus from your life back to family dynamics. By saying "your father," you’re acknowledging my role as the third party in this dialogue.

10. **ADULT CHILD:** (Nods, not looking at them) Yeah. It’s a lot of change for everyone.

**SUBTEXT (ADULT CHILD):** *Final statement.* A total non-sequitur and a complete evasive answer to every unspoken question on the table. The "for everyone" is an inclusive gesture that includes no one specific—the ultimate passive-aggressive nod. The silence that follows stretches into a full minute, filled only with the clink of cutlery against plates and the distant hum of the refrigerator.

The parent has said nothing about their own life, only asked vague questions about stability and work. They have mentioned family memories, framed the child’s choices as uncertain ("adjustment period"), and then dropped a casual mention of their own retirement adjustment to shift the focus. The adult child has given evasive answers that maintain a polite surface while refusing every actual connection—the "fine," the "yeah," the "it might not be the best timing." Each line is a layer of subtext, with meaning buried in tone and phrasing rather than explicit declaration. They are having two completely different conversations in the same room.

## p3_chase_uneven_terrain

**Prompt:** Write a foot chase across a rooftop under construction, with exposed beams and unfinished flooring. Track exact footing, balance, and near-falls for both the pursuer and the pursued. Avoid cinematic flourish; focus on kinesthetic detail.

### Response A

Kael sprinted across the rooftop, his sneakers pounding against the rough-hewn planks of the unfinished flooring. The wooden slats creaked beneath him as he went, sending tiny tremors through his ankles with each step.

He dodged a loose bundle of rebar, narrowly avoiding a stumble as it rolled away from his feet. The metallic tang of fresh steel filled the air, mingling with the scent of wet concrete and dust.

Behind him, Victor's footsteps echoed through the narrow corridor between two rows of steel framing. Victor's own sneakers made no attempt to soften their impact; every step was a firm declaration of pursuit.

As Kael reached the midpoint of the rooftop, he spotted a gap in the flooring – a section where the boards had been ripped up to expose a tangle of pipes and wiring below. He veered left, his footwork swift but not frantic. For an instant, his weight bore down on the wooden slats, threatening to snap them under his impact.

Victor was closing in now, his heavy breathing audible over the rustling of the wind. Kael's eyes darted between the pursuer and the gap ahead, gauging his chances for escape.

He took another stride forward, this time placing himself more carefully on a single board that wobbled ominously under his weight. For an instant, he teetered, his center of gravity tilted, before readjusting his stance to balance on two adjacent boards.

A jolt rocked him as Victor's outstretched hand brushed against the loose rebar bundle, sending it clattering across the rooftop. The sudden disturbance sent Kael stumbling – a small misstep that threatened to turn into a full-blown fall.

He caught himself on a nearby beam, fingers scrabbling for purchase on the slick metal surface as he regained his balance. For a heartbeat, his weight hung in mid-air, and then he was down again, sprinting across the rooftop with renewed urgency.

Victor closed within a few strides behind, his hands grasping at Kael's back like a pair of vise-like claws. Kael felt Victor's grip slip for an instant – just long enough to yank free from his grasp and launch himself into another burst of speed.

This time, he aimed straight for the gap in the flooring ahead. His right foot landed squarely on one plank, while his left toe clipped a loose edge and sent him skittering sideways across the rooftop. Only sheer luck kept him upright as he slid past a metal railing, nearly toppling into empty space.

He pulls himself up and forward until his knees are on the beam. For a moment, they face each other on opposite sides of this narrow metal bridge, the only sounds the creak of the structure and their heavy breathing.

Then Alistair pushes upward with one leg against the steel, propelling him to a sprint across the final distance. The end of the scaffolding is just ahead.

## p4_quiet_introspection

**Prompt:** Write a short quiet scene of a character packing a childhood bedroom into boxes after a parent's death. Ground the scene in specific physical objects and actions rather than internal monologue about grief.

### Response A

The air in the bedroom is thick with dust and memory. The windows are sealed, but the smell of old books and damp wool seeps through the cracks.

Aila stands at the foot of her childhood bed, a single box open on the floor before her. It’s labeled *Kitchen* in sharp black marker. She begins by collecting the scattered toys from beneath the bed: a faded pink ball, a wooden puzzle missing two pieces, a plastic unicorn with a cracked horn. Each object is placed gently into the box. The plastic unicorn is positioned upright against the side of the cardboard wall, its one remaining piece—a delicate filigree tail—facing outward as if it might still be ridden.

Next are the clothes. The closet door hangs crookedly from its hinges, but she opens it slowly. She doesn’t need to touch them. The first garment is a yellow cotton dress with a white apron tied at the back, hanging like a ghost. It’s not an outfit Aila ever wore; her mother had sewn it for someone else long ago. Aila places it in the *Kitchen* box without unpinning it from the hanger.

Her eyes skip over the framed photos on the dresser: herself at age eight, grinning with crooked front teeth and a hand full of dandelion fluff; her parents beaming together, their faces smooth against time. She leaves them. The task is not preservation but extraction. She focuses on the objects that held her world together before they became mere photographs.

She moves to the nightstand, where her mother’s watch—its leather strap worn white at the buckle—is set next to a small ceramic rabbit Aila had painted in primary school. Both are placed into a separate *Miscellaneous* box she hadn’t opened yet, alongside a single, dried lavender stem and a bent paperclip. The watch is now just another thing with no owner.

She crosses the room to the bookshelf. Each shelf was divided by a thin ribbon of wallpaper, held down by small ceramic tiles that had been painted in different colors: red for fiction, blue for nonfiction. The books are arranged alphabetically within their categories. Aila takes only two volumes. *The Wind in the Willows* is placed with its back cover up in the *Kitchen* box. It’s a family favorite, but the illustrations were already faded when she was eight. The other book is *The Collected Poems of Emily Dickinson*. Its hardcover is dented on the spine, and the pages smell of damp paper towels from an accident long past. She places it in the *Miscellaneous* box with the watch, the rabbit, and the lavender stem.

She stops by her mother’s chair, where a half-finished cross-stitch project hangs lopsided over the armrest—a red cardinal on a green branch, one eye still bare white wool. Aila does not touch the fabric. She lifts the chair, dust flying in the pale afternoon light that cuts through the slats of the blinds, and sets it atop the stack of *Kitchen* boxes already there.

The last object is her mother’s silver locket. It rests on a small porcelain dish from a tea set Aila had received when she was nine. The dish has a crack running from rim to foot, but its white is still intact in this one spot where it touches the locket. Aila carefully picks up both objects and places them into a *Personal* box with her own name on it—red ink, childish—already packed at the top of the stack.

She stands back and looks at the room. The furniture is stripped bare: no books on the shelves, no toys under the bed, no clothes in the closet except for two dresses that hung too long and are now folded on a shelf to await a new home. Her own suitcase sits open against the dresser. It’s filled with socks, t-shirts, and her current laptop charger from college, but she sees it as empty space still waiting.

She begins packing again. The next box is for *Bedding*, then *Living Room*. The rhythm of the movement becomes the only sound in the quiet: the soft thud of a small ceramic tile into a *Kitchen* box, the rustle of a dry wool apron, the click of a paperclip being bent back into place. Her movements are precise, economical, devoid of sentiment. She is not sorting her past; she is cataloging objects for transfer.

The dust motes in the sunbeams move lazily on the air between each box.

## p5_negotiation_standoff

**Prompt:** Write a tense standoff between a shop owner and a debt collector across a counter. Track hand positions, what each is holding or reaching for, and the physical objects between them. Keep dialogue clipped and avoid melodrama.

### Response A

The shop owner, Mrs. Patel, stood behind the counter, her hands gripping the edge of it tightly. Her eyes locked onto the debt collector, Mr. Jenkins, who had his back against the door frame. A stack of unpaid bills lay between them on the counter, each one creased and dog-eared.

"Payment's past due," Mr. Jenkins said, his voice firm but not aggressive. He reached for a small notebook in his breast pocket with his right hand, leaving his left resting on the door frame.

Mrs. Patel's eyes flicked to the notebook before returning to Mr. Jenkins' face. "I know. I've been trying to get some extra work in."

Mr. Jenkins didn't react, but his fingers tightened around the pen stuck between his notebook and a folder full of papers. "We can discuss payment plans if you'd like."

Mrs. Patel's grip on the counter tightened as she leaned forward slightly. "I don't think that'll be necessary."

Mr. Jenkins' left hand shifted from the door frame to rest on the edge of the counter, inches from Mrs. Patel's right elbow. The tension between them was palpable.

"I'm afraid it is," he said, his voice steady. "We can't just...forgive debt."

Mrs. Patel's eyes darted to the phone on the wall behind Mr. Jenkins before returning to him. She was considering her next move – whether to call someone for backup or try to reason with Mr. Jenkins.

The air between them hung heavy, each waiting for the other to make a move. The only sound was the soft hum of the fluorescent lights overhead and the quiet rustling of papers in Mr. Jenkins' folder.

For what felt like an eternity, nothing changed. Then Mrs. Patel spoke up, her voice calm but firm. "I'll need some time to look into it."

Mr. Jenkins nodded once before straightening up from against the door frame. He slid his notebook back into his pocket and stepped forward, leaving a small gap between them on the counter.

"I'll leave my card," he said, laying a business card next to the stack of unpaid bills. "You can reach me if you need to discuss further."

Without another word, Mr. Jenkins turned and walked out of the shop, leaving Mrs. Patel standing behind the counter, her grip still firm on its edge.

### Response B

**The Afternoon Counter**

The store was quiet, the only sound the low hum of refrigeration in the back room. The scent of fresh coffee beans from this morning still hung over the worn linoleum, but it couldn’t mask the dry, papery smell of unpaid invoices and late notices.

Shop Owner, Mr. Patel, sat on his side of the counter. His hands rested palm-up, fingers lightly splayed on a stack of credit card receipts he’d just counted. The cash register lay before him, its keys cool to the touch in the still air. Behind him, a shelf held neatly organized rows of cleaning supplies, their labels facing inward.

Across the worn laminate, Debt Collector, Ms. Kaelin, stood with her weight on one leg, the other foot planted flat on the counter’s surface as if ready to pivot or make an abrupt reach. She held a manila folder in one hand, its cover creased from repeated use. The other hand was empty but hovered near a small, locked drawer at waist level. Her eyes tracked the path of Mr. Patel’s hands.

Mr. Patel didn’t look up immediately. He scanned his own stack, then let his gaze drift to Ms. Kaelin. When he met her eye, he nodded once, curtly, before picking up the phone receiver and placing it on its cradle with a definitive *click*. The sudden stillness felt thicker than before.

“Your last notice was dated four weeks ago,” Mr. Patel said, his voice calm but final, not inviting conversation. “We agreed to an extension then. I expect you to respect that.”

Ms. Kaelin didn’t move. She tilted her head a millimeter, her gaze flicking from Mr. Patel’s hands to the stack of receipts behind them. Her hand in the manila folder shifted, resting it on the edge of the counter. “The agreement was for thirty days,” she countered. “That window has long since passed. The client is escalating their own complaints.”

“The client is a family-owned business,” Mr. Patel corrected quietly, his fingers drumming a slow, deliberate pattern on the receipts. *Tap-tap*, the only sound in the quiet space. He didn’t look up from the papers. “They are not your usual clients. They respect community, and they will hear about how you operate.”

Ms. Kaelin’s expression was neutral, but her breathing became slightly audible against the stillness. Her hand tightened around the manila folder’s corner, a subtle flex of tendons at the wrist.

“The client is,” Ms. Kaelin said, the word flat and final, “a business with obligations to us and our investors.”

“Then,” Mr. Patel stood, slowly rising from his stool like an anchor being lifted, “then we have a different conversation.” His right hand swept across the counter’s surface, clearing a narrow space of invoices and deposit slips. He placed both palms flat on this new, open space between them—a clear territorial marker.

Ms. Kaelin matched him in height. Her left hand, the one hovering by the locked drawer, reached forward just enough to tap three times against the manila folder’s cover, a quiet, rhythmic punctuation of her words. She did not move from her position blocking the counter door to the shop’s back room.

“The extension is over,” Ms. Kaelin said, each word sharp and clear in the space. “The payment was overdue by your own account.”

“Your notice was incorrect.” Mr. Patel’s eyes never left hers but moved to a point just past her shoulder, as if mentally measuring the distance between them and the shop door. His left hand rested lightly on his thigh, near the hem of his trousers.

“You received it,” Ms. Kaelin replied, the statement not an argument. She looked past Mr. Patel’s right ear at something on the shelf behind him—the cleaning supplies perhaps—but her attention was still fully focused on the space between them. The folder remained clutched in her right hand, a constant weight.

A heavy silence settled over the counter. It wasn’t empty; it hummed with the tension of two bodies angled like pawns. Each exhaled a breath that did not reach the other side. Mr. Patel’s left foot tapped once—a slow, deliberate echo—before settling back to its place on the stool’s floor.

Then Ms. Kaelin moved. Her right hand lifted from the folder’s corner and hovered in the space between them for a moment before she reached into the pocket of her jacket with her empty left hand. She produced a single, thick envelope bearing a corporate logo and a date stamp several days older than today’s notice. She placed it on the counter’s surface, centered between Mr. Patel’s palms.

“It is your choice,” Ms. Kaelin said, her voice still, as if she were speaking to a piece of furniture. “The payment. In full.” The manila folder remained closed in her left hand, its cover still creased from use. Her eyes were fixed on the envelope she’d just placed down, not on Mr. Patel.

Mr. Patel’s hands were now empty. They hovered over the cash register’s keys for a heartbeat before he looked down at the space between them—first at the envelope Ms. Kaelin had left, then up to her face and the folder in her hand. The tension did not dissipate; it became crystalline.

He said nothing. He made no move. His posture remained neutral but still, his hands poised, ready.

Ms. Kaelin did not look at him now either. She stared straight into the empty space between them, a fixed point on nothing and everything all at once. The air was thick with unspoken threat in her presence, a quiet pressure that felt like an unseen weight bearing down. Neither touched the envelope. It sat there, a small island of corporate authority and financial obligation.

The standoff persisted: two people facing each other across the scarred counter, their hands poised, no sound but the hum of the refrigerator.
